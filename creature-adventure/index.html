My son designed a game, and I was the translator.

Creature Adventure is a side-scrolling creature runner for the iPad, designed by my nine-year-old and built by the two of us in one long day, with Claude as our coding teacher. He made the design calls. I turned them into requests, and Claude explained the why before it wrote anything.

Where it started

The focus room taught me to draw everything in code.

Earlier this month I built Pit Wall Focus Room, and the biggest lesson from that build was that art drawn in code can be changed just by talking about it. The garage, the crew, and the car are all shapes, so every time I changed my mind it took a conversation instead of a new set of images.

That's what made me think we could build a 2D game the same way. My son's question was the obvious one: could we build one for HIM? I brought him a rough pitch (a creature that runs, jumps, collects things, and grows up along the way), and everything after that is mostly his.

We also looked at the prompt-to-game tools that build a whole game from a description, and passed on them for the real build. They skip the part where he learns how it works, and they didn't fit the plan of a plain static site on GitHub.

How we designed it

He made the calls, and I turned them into requests.

Asking a nine-year-old open questions gets you a lot of very fast talking. So I built him an interview page with checkboxes – pick the feel, pick the creatures, pick the controls – and we recorded the conversation while he went through it. Every answer gave us something to talk about, and every decision below came out of that conversation.

  1. THE NAME

    He turned down "Dash."

    I suggested a name with "Dash" in it. He said no, because it "sounds like there's no evolving."

  2. THE OBSTACLES

    No puddles.

    Water creatures would just walk through them, so what's the point?

  3. THE BELLY MARK

    He caught the flame twice.

    The first flame mark on the Fire creature looked like a water drop, and he said so right away. Once it pointed up, he noticed it sat so close to the mouth that it looked wrong, so the creatures got taller to give every type's mark room in the middle of the belly.

  4. THE BODY

    Bat wings and circle hands.

    The first wings were little triangles. He asked for bat wings, plus circle hands and circle feet, and the creature is still one round blob underneath.

  5. THE WORLDS

    A world for every type, and an outfit for each one.

    He picked all six (Volcano, Ocean, Spring Forest, Thunderstorm, Treasure Cave, Mountains) and dressed the trainer for each: a firefighter's helmet at the volcano, scuba gear in the ocean, a rain jacket in the storm, a wizard hat with a light on top in the cave, a hard hat in the mountains.

  6. THE CONTROLS

    Tap and double-tap. Nothing else.

    He was firm on that from the start, and every idea since has been tested against it.

Where we disagreed

We disagreed twice, and both times the game got better.

The music tracks run about two and a half minutes, so I wanted every level timed to last about two minutes. He hated it, and told me exactly why:

"Every time there is minutes, it always rushes me."

He was right. Levels are a set distance now, with a progress bar and a flag at the finish and no clock anywhere on the screen.

The other one was mine to set: no weapons in this game. Instead of arguing, he turned it into something better. Creatures fill an energy bar and use their OWN moves on the boss, and the friends you rescue along the way hand you potions and gear for the fight.

What he learned

He watched what he said out loud turn into the game.

He'd say an idea, and within minutes it was a working piece of the game he could play. That's the picture of AI I wanted him to have: you say what you want, and you get to watch how it gets built.

Two things in the game are there just for that. The knobs file holds the numbers, colors, and move names the lead designer is allowed to change himself, so he changes a number, reloads, and sees what happens (sometimes that's a creature flying off the top of the screen). And the code monitor is a screen in the sky that shows the game's real code and lights up the line that runs when you jump, rescue a friend, or hit the boss.

What's in it

A static site, a few small files, and no accounts.

It's plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript on GitHub Pages: no game engine, no server, no sign-in. It plays on an iPad and on a computer.

ADVENTURE
Three worlds, each with four levels and then a boss. In the levels, you land on minions to free captured friends, and each friend's home becomes a checkpoint. Rescued friends give you items for the boss fight. Bosses send high and low attacks, and a full energy bar fires your creature's move at the boss's weak spots. Your creature Powers Up after each boss.
ZEN
Endless and calm, with no way to lose and a best distance to beat.
NAMES
Players can name their creature or keep the type name. His idea.
THE ART
Every creature, world, and trainer is drawn in code: circles, triangles, and curves. All original.
THE MUSIC
Ten tracks I made with Lyria in the Gemini app, reused from the focus room.
THE CODE
About 1,100 lines across seven small JavaScript files, each with one job: the knobs, the creatures, the trainer, the worlds, the levels, the bosses, and one file that ties it together.
SAVES
Progress, names, and best distance stay in the browser on your own device. Nothing gets sent anywhere.
Process notes

A few things from launch day.

I'm learning to code alongside him, so Claude worked as a teacher more than a producer: it explained what each piece did before writing it, and we decided together what went in next. Before it shipped, the game was tested with an automated "robot player" that ran through it on its own.

Putting it on GitHub went wrong twice. GitHub's website rejected my first upload – every file was under the limit, but I'd dragged in all 40 MB as one commit – so we split it, with the code first and the music in its own commit. Then a couple of files went into the wrong folder and broke the How to play button, so Claude connected to the repo and moved them itself.

He's already planning the next one.

He has more game ideas, he wants to build a 3D game next, and he's started talking about being a game designer. He's 9½, and he's already thinking about what he could do with this technology.

That's the part I care about most: the creativity this opens up for him, and what the two of us can build together.
