How I got here

I used ChatGPT like a better Google, and kept going back to Google.

That went on for about a year and a half. Every time I tried it I ended up back where I started, because Google was what I knew and it was easy. It also never touched the shape of the job I actually did — we had no AI tooling at work at all, so everything I learned early on, I learned at home.

July 2025 is the date I can point to. My manager at Recorded Future sent me a Reddit post about using Gemini's Gem Builder to interview me — to have it ask the questions instead of waiting for instructions. That's when I started telling it who I was: my background, my expertise, my writing samples, my resume. The model didn't get smarter. It got context.

The next six months I built constantly. A customer success email planner trained on hundreds of the lifecycle emails we'd actually sent. Something to get the cursor moving on a webinar invitation. A place to log what I'd shipped so I wasn't reconstructing a quarter from memory at review time. I'd hand it a sheet of email performance and get the aggregates back instead of doing month-over-month by hand.

Then the thing that made it useful broke it. I'd given it so much about me that it started using all of it, everywhere. I cook with cast iron, so it brought up cast iron constantly. I play Destiny, so everything turned into a Destiny metaphor. That isn't hallucination — it's context applied where nobody asked for it, and it's the failure mode I design against now.

February 2026 I moved to Claude and started building projects, and what changed was that things persisted. Now Claude writes the research prompts, Gemini runs the deep research, and Claude synthesizes it, checks the sources, and cold-reads its own conclusions before any of it lands in an instruction set.

Design time vs. runtime

The harder you work at design time, the lighter the model you need at runtime.

I don't want to update an instruction set every time something changes. So the only things in mine are the parts that don't move: how the system works, where to go looking, the rules it can't break. Everything else lives in a grounding source it reads when it needs to and writes back to when something changes. Notion for me, SharePoint for the knowledge system I stood up at Henry Schein One, Drive or a notebook if you're on Gemini.

There's still reasoning happening at runtime. The difference is that the rules already decided it. The instructions and the grounding source together supply the logic, so the model is applying a decision rather than making a fresh one and hoping it matches the last one.

The small version: I store one date of birth and have the system calculate age against today. I've never edited that field again.

Where it clicked was a business-review prep prompt I'd shipped to the Gainsight Copilot library at Henry Schein One. It re-reasoned the whole script on every run, so formatting drifted and talking points dropped, and CSMs were re-prompting to get something complete. I'd made their job harder, so I axed it. Our BI and data teams and I rebuilt the logic into a Domo dashboard — enter an account, get the same complete output every time.

The home version made the cost argument for me. Designing the recipe-card system took a few hours of back-and-forth on a heavy model: writing the rules, breaking it, writing more rules. That horsepower got spent once. The cards get built on a much lighter model now, and they're still detailed enough to cook from. That math matters a lot more when the thing you're building gets used by fifty CSMs instead of one person.

Permissioning

What the tool is allowed to reach decides what you can build.

At Recorded Future I rolled out Gainsight's Slack AI agent to the CSM team, replacing a notification-only app. Piloted with under ten people first. Access inherited each rep's Salesforce permissions, so a CSM saw their own book and a manager saw what rolled up to them — no second permission model to maintain.

Clearing legal and InfoSec at a threat intelligence company was never going to be a formality. It meant working with Gainsight's product team to confirm a wildcard operator that confined the agent to DMs and kept it out of every channel, public and private.

Henry Schein One is the more useful half of the story. I wanted a Copilot Studio agent with a live line into Gainsight, and we couldn't get MCP access into Microsoft Copilot at all. The answer that came back was to use what a vendor already offered.

So we rerouted. Gainsight's own Copilot already reaches the account data, which covers the account questions without asking anyone for anything. For everything else we built declarative Microsoft Copilot agents grounded solely in SharePoint knowledge libraries — call recordings, SOPs, knowledge docs, and a list of external-facing product links. Because Copilot is grounded in the Microsoft Graph, a CSM with Copilot Premium can search across Outlook, Teams, and SharePoint using an LLM security had already approved. The agent can't touch Gainsight or Salesforce. It doesn't need to.

We got blocked, found the workaround, and the workaround turned out to be nearly as useful — the CSMs got a real search tool out of it. Ask for the thing you actually want. Then go find out what you're allowed to reach, because that answer is going to shape the architecture either way.

Failure discipline

Build it, watch it break, add the rule.

Ten bugs in a single session, all found the same way: build a card, open it on my phone, screenshot whatever's wrong. A recipe where the title and the body text both rendered dark-on-dark and disappeared, because I keep my phone in dark mode and the card assumed I wouldn't. Raw HTML dumped into the chat as plain text, because the skill didn't resolve and the build quietly fell back to something else. A card that claimed it saved my progress and lost it the second I closed it.

I caught the dark-mode one because of email operations. Getting a message to render across clients that each make their own decisions about your CSS is most of the job in email design, and dark mode is where the majority of it goes wrong. That instinct came with me.

None of it was caught by the system. There's no automated check that tells you the text is invisible on the device someone is actually going to read it on. I found it by testing, then handed the screenshot back and asked what went wrong.

Fixing the card in front of me was never the point. Each one went back into the skill as a permanent rule, so the next build can't reintroduce it. One declared palette, never two. Every JS lookup null-safe. If a button can't reliably do the thing, don't draw the button.

The one I liked best wasn't a defect at all. A card put sauce-whisking at step three, over a hot skillet, because that's where the source recipe had it. Nothing was broken — the order just never got checked. That fix isn't a CSS rule, it's a habit: read the whole recipe before you start, or you get to the part where the sauce goes in and think, oh crud, I need the sauce. So now the skill has to write out a sequencing pass before it writes any HTML. Do it the way I'd do it.

Amplification, not replacement.

The point was never doing more work. It's that I keep coming back to build the next thing as inspiration strikes, and every one of them hands time back.

Some of it is plain scheduling. Both kids are at the same school now, so a skill scans their teachers' emails and posts one update to Slack — the dentist appointment, the travel week, the fact that I need to book a kennel for the dog. I stopped carrying that around in my head.

The part I'd defend hardest is the cold read. I'll talk through a hard conversation afterward and what I get back isn't agreement. It's: you may be reading that as nitpicking, and here's the other side of it. That lets me stand outside a conversation and see the whole shape of it. Same with this page — I talked it through out loud for half an hour instead of typing, and got pushed on a good portion of it.

It's a second brain that argues with me. It asks what I left out. It doesn't tell me what I want to hear.

I'm still the human in the loop, and I get more time with my family. That's what makes the difference.
